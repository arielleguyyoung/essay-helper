## 1. Decoding the question

The question asks three different things:

1. **"Can we distinguish...?"** Conceptual and descriptive. Is there a coherent line at all? Where has it been drawn, and by whom?
2. **"...and should we?"** Normative. Even if the line is artificial, is it worth keeping? You can say "not cleanly" to part 1 and "yes, for some purposes" to part 2. That combination is probably the strongest position available.
3. **"...and keep politics out of the private?"** A separate claim hidden in the question: that the private is a zone the state may not enter. You can accept the distinction and still reject this. Domestic violence is the obvious test.

**The trap:** answering only "can we" (a feminist critique ending in "the line is a myth") or only "should we" (a privacy defence that never asks where the line came from). Answer all three explicitly.

**Define in the introduction:**

- **Public.** At least three senses: the state and its coercive power (Locke, Mill); the space where citizens deliberate together (Arendt, Habermas); and the market/civil society, which liberals often class as *private* against the state. Weintraub (1997) maps these.
- **Private.** Household/family; conscience and body; intimate relationships; personal information. Bodily privacy (abortion) and data privacy (surveillance) raise different arguments.
- **Politics.** The hinge of the essay. If politics = *state power*, keeping it out of the private is a coherent liberal goal. If politics = *power relations of any kind* (feminist and Foucauldian sense), the private already contains politics, so "keeping it out" makes no sense. Say which sense the question uses, then show what changes when you switch.

"Can we and should we" calls for evaluation: reach a judgement, don't just survey positions.

---

## 2. Thesis options

**A. Qualified liberal defence.** The distinction is coherent understood as a limit on legitimate state coercion, not a description of places. Mill's harm principle gives a workable test; politics stays out except where harm to others is at stake.
*Strength:* clean, defensible with Mill and Rawls. *Weakness:* "harm" isn't neutral. Marital rape wasn't recognised as a crime in England until *R v R* (1991).

**B. Feminist critique.** The distinction can't be drawn neutrally; it was built to keep relations between men and women out of political scrutiny. "The personal is political" (Hanisch 1970) is a diagnosis: politics cannot and should not be kept out.
*Strength:* strong literature (Pateman, Okin, MacKinnon). *Weakness:* struggles with "should". If nothing is private, there's no principled ground for resisting state intrusion into sexuality, reproduction or personal data, which many feminists want (abortion rights, LGBT rights).

**C. Middle position (recommended).** There is no natural line; where it falls is a political decision, and historically it has been drawn to shield domination in the household. But the distinction is worth keeping as a contestable, revisable protection for individual *decisional and informational* privacy. What should be rejected is the claim that the private is a zone the state may never enter: the state must act inside the home to prevent domination (domestic abuse, coercive control), while staying out of choices about intimacy, reproduction and conscience.

**Why C:** it's the only one that answers all three parts separately — *can* (not naturally, but yes as a political construction), *should* (yes, for specified purposes), *keep politics out* (no, not as a blanket rule). It treats both the liberal and feminist positions as real interlocutors, which is where critical-evaluation marks are. Okin (1989) and Fraser (1990) are close to it. Avoid sounding like "both sides have a point" by stating precisely what the line protects (decisions and information about one's own body, intimacy, conscience) and what it doesn't (power exercised over another person in the household).

## 3. Paragraph-by-paragraph outline (~2,300 words)

### Introduction (~250 words)
- **Open concretely.** Until 1991 a husband in England could not be convicted of raping his wife, because marriage was taken to imply consent (*R v R* [1991] UKHL 12). One legal fact shows the public/private line doing political work.
- Define public, private, politics (state power vs power relations).
- Thesis C in two sentences.
- One-sentence roadmap.

### Section 1. Where the distinction comes from (~350 words)
- **Claim:** the distinction is old but has never meant one thing; each version was built for a purpose.
- **Evidence:**
  - Aristotle, *Politics* Book I: *oikos* (necessity; rule over wife, children, slaves) vs *polis* (free equals deliberating). The private is the lower sphere.
  - Locke, *Second Treatise* ch. VI: separates political power from paternal/conjugal power (against Filmer). The founding liberal move, and the one Pateman attacks.
  - Arendt, *The Human Condition* (1958): "private" carries the sense of *privation*; worries "the social" has absorbed the public realm.
  - Habermas, *Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*: private people coming together as a public to reason about the state.
- **Analysis:** the value attached to each side flips. For Aristotle and Arendt the public is freedom; for Locke and later liberals the private is freedom. A distinction whose evaluation can reverse isn't a fact of nature.
- **Link:** we *can* draw the line, but there's no single line, so the question is who draws it and why.

### Section 2. The feminist critique: the private as a site of power (~400 words)
- **Claim:** the liberal distinction hid domination by labelling the household pre-political.
- **Evidence:**
  - Pateman, "Feminist Critiques of the Public/Private Dichotomy" (1983); *The Sexual Contract* (1988): the social contract presupposes a prior sexual contract subordinating women in the private sphere.
  - Hanisch, "The Personal Is Political" (1970): "personal" problems are shared, structural, political.
  - MacKinnon, "Privacy v. Equality" in *Feminism Unmodified* (1987): the "right to be left alone" can mean women left alone with their abusers.
  - Examples: the marital rape exemption; unpaid care work (Waring, *If Women Counted*, 1988). [VERIFY: if you use a current ONS figure on the value of unpaid household work, find the latest release yourself.]
- **Analysis:** if politics means power relations, the family is political whatever the state does. State *non*-intervention is itself a political choice: it leaves existing power where it is.
- **Link:** the line is politically constructed — "can we?" → "not neutrally".

### Section 3. Justice inside the family: Rawls and Okin (~350 words)
- **Claim:** even liberal theory has had to admit the family into the scope of justice.
- **Evidence:**
  - Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): the family is part of the basic structure, but assumed just; parties modelled as heads of households.
  - Okin, *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989): if the family is in the basic structure, justice applies to it. The gendered division of labour creates unequal vulnerability (esp. at divorce), and the family is where children learn justice.
  - Rawls, "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" (1997): justice doesn't regulate the family's internal life directly but constrains it and protects members' basic rights.
- **Analysis:** Rawls's reply is close to thesis C. Does it go far enough? Okin: it leaves the division of labour untouched. Reply: that needs policy (parental leave, childcare), not the abolition of privacy.
- **Link:** shifts from "can we" to "should we" — what is the line *for*?

### Section 4. Why we should still keep a private sphere (~400 words)
- **Claim:** the critique shows the line is political, not that it's worthless. Feminist and LGBT movements have themselves relied on it.
- **Evidence:**
  - Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) ch. I, harm principle [check wording in your edition]; *The Subjection of Women* (1869) shows Mill didn't treat the family as beyond justice.
  - Hart–Devlin debate after the Wolfenden Report (1957): Devlin, *The Enforcement of Morals* (1965) vs Hart, *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963). Decriminalising private homosexual acts: the private sphere protecting a minority.
  - Reproductive rights: *Griswold* (1965) and *Roe* (1973) grounded in privacy; *Dobbs* (2022) overturned *Roe* — showing both the fragility of privacy-based rights and what's lost when the private shrinks. Bring MacKinnon back: equality grounding might be sturdier.
  - Same-sex marriage: *Lawrence v Texas* (2003) used privacy; *Obergefell* (2015) and the UK Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 demanded *public* recognition. Sometimes the goal is to make the private public.
  - Data privacy: Snowden (2013); Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) — the threat now comes from corporations too. Rössler, *The Value of Privacy* (2005): privacy secures autonomy.
- **Analysis:** what deserves protection is not *places* (the home) but *kinds of decision and information*. This protects the person, not the household head.
- **Link:** "should we?" → yes, for specified purposes.

### Section 5. Against "keeping politics out": domestic abuse and the state (~300 words)
- **Claim:** once the private means decisional/informational privacy, not the household, there's no principled bar to state intervention in the home — and a strong duty of it.
- **Evidence:**
  - *R v R* (1991).
  - Controlling or coercive behaviour offence, Serious Crime Act 2015 s. 76; Domestic Abuse Act 2021. [VERIFY on legislation.gov.uk]
  - Foucault, *The History of Sexuality* vol. 1 (1976/1978): sexuality and the family are already governed through medicine, psychiatry, norms (biopower). The private was never empty of power; the choice is *which* power and whether it's accountable.
- **Analysis:** coercive control law protects the victim's private life by invading the abuser's. That only makes sense if privacy belongs to persons, not households — the core of thesis C.
- **Link:** "keep politics out?" → no, not as a blanket rule.

### Section 6. Counterargument and rebuttal (~250 words)
Give the strongest objection from the table below (probably the slippery slope or the Arendt objection) a full paragraph.

### Conclusion (~200 words)
- One sentence per part: can we (yes, but only as a political choice); should we (yes, for decisional and informational privacy); keep politics out (no).
- Add something new: the hardest current cases (data, platforms) involve private corporations, which neither the classic liberal nor feminist version was built for. Leave it as an open problem.

## 4. Counterarguments and rebuttals

| Objection | Who | Rebuttal |
|---|---|---|
| If the line is constructed, it's arbitrary and should be dropped. | Strong reading of Hanisch/MacKinnon | Constructed ≠ arbitrary; rights and borders are constructed and still do work. Coercive control law shows the line can be revised to protect persons rather than patriarchs. |
| Letting the state into the family invites overreach (morals policing, surveillance of parenting). | Mill, Hart | Agree and build it in: the test is domination of one member by another, not departure from approved norms. Avoid Devlin; the harm principle still filters. |
| The household is governed by affection, not justice. | Communitarian; Sandel, *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) [VERIFY pages] | Okin: justice is the fallback when affection fails — divorce, abuse — exactly when it matters most. |
| Privacy is the wrong basis for reproductive/sexual rights. | MacKinnon | Partly concede (*Dobbs*), but equality and privacy arguments can be held together. |
| The map is outdated: the main threat is corporations. | Zuboff; Habermas on "refeudalisation" | Accept it and use it in the conclusion: the line must be redrawn politically, not inherited. |
| Politicising everything destroys the public realm ("the social"). | Arendt (1958) | The least obvious objection — markers will notice. Reply via Fraser (1990): what counts as a common concern is itself for public debate, so it can't be settled in advance by excluding the household. |
