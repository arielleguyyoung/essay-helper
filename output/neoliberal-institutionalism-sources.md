# Sources: How convincing is neoliberal institutionalism?

Harvard style. The research librarian confirmed every citation through publisher pages, library catalogues or RePEc. Check DOIs marked "unconfirmed" at doi.org before you submit. **[CORE]** marks the 15 essential sources.

## 1. Core neoliberal institutionalism
- **[CORE]** Keohane, R.O. (1984) *After Hegemony: Cooperation and Discord in the World Political Economy*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. States use regimes to cooperate without a hegemon because regimes lower transaction costs and reduce uncertainty. *Supports; this is your anchor text.*
- **[CORE]** Keohane, R.O. and Nye, J.S. (1977) *Power and Interdependence: World Politics in Transition*. Boston: Little, Brown. (4th edn 2012, Longman.) Sets out complex interdependence. *Supports; also shows the theory's scope limits.*
- **[CORE]** Axelrod, R. and Keohane, R.O. (1985) 'Achieving cooperation under anarchy: strategies and institutions', *World Politics*, 38(1), pp. 226–254. (DOI unconfirmed; free PDF on Axelrod's Michigan page.) Cooperation depends on how much interests overlap, the shadow of the future and the number of actors. *Supports; this is the game-theory core.*
- **[CORE]** Keohane, R.O. and Martin, L.L. (1995) 'The promise of institutionalist theory', *International Security*, 20(1), pp. 39–51. doi:10.2307/2539214. A reply to Mearsheimer. *Supports.*

## 2. Realist critiques
- **[CORE]** Waltz, K.N. (1979) *Theory of International Politics*. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley. The neorealist baseline.
- **[CORE]** Grieco, J.M. (1988) 'Anarchy and the limits of cooperation: a realist critique of the newest liberal institutionalism', *International Organization*, 42(3), pp. 485–507. doi:10.1017/S0020818300027715. Argues states are defensive positionalists who care about relative gains. *Critiques.*
- **[CORE]** Mearsheimer, J.J. (1994/95) 'The false promise of international institutions', *International Security*, 19(3), pp. 5–49. doi:10.2307/2539078. Institutions reflect the distribution of power. *Critiques.*

## 3. Relative vs absolute gains
- **[CORE]** Powell, R. (1991) 'Absolute and relative gains in international relations theory', *American Political Science Review*, 85(4), pp. 1303–1320. doi:10.2307/1963947. Relative gains matter when force is cheap. *Middle ground.*
- **[CORE]** Snidal, D. (1991) 'Relative gains and the pattern of international cooperation', *American Political Science Review*, 85(3), pp. 701–726. (DOI unconfirmed.) Relative-gains concerns fade when more than two actors are involved. *Narrows Grieco's critique.*
- **[CORE]** Baldwin, D.A. (ed.) (1993) *Neorealism and Neoliberalism: The Contemporary Debate*. New York: Columbia University Press. Overview of the debate.

## 4. Constructivist and critical critiques
- **[CORE]** Wendt, A. (1992) 'Anarchy is what states make of it: the social construction of power politics', *International Organization*, 46(2), pp. 391–425. (DOI unconfirmed.) Interests are not given. *Critiques.*
- **[CORE]** Ruggie, J.G. (1982) 'International regimes, transactions, and change: embedded liberalism in the postwar economic order', *International Organization*, 36(2), pp. 379–415. doi:10.1017/S0020818300018993. Regimes rest on a shared social purpose. *Critiques.*
- **[CORE]** Barnett, M.N. and Finnemore, M. (1999) 'The politics, power, and pathologies of international organizations', *International Organization*, 53(4), pp. 699–732. (DOI unconfirmed.) International organisations act autonomously and develop pathologies. *Critiques.*

## 5. Evidence and cases
- **[CORE]** Ikenberry, G.J. (2001) *After Victory: Institutions, Strategic Restraint, and the Rebuilding of Order after Major Wars*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. Institutional self-binding explains why the post-1945 order lasted.
- **[CORE]** Wallander, C.A. (2000) 'Institutional assets and adaptability: NATO after the Cold War', *International Organization*, 54(4), pp. 705–735. (DOI unconfirmed.) NATO survived when realists predicted it would not.
- **[CORE]** Goldstein, J.L., Rivers, D. and Tomz, M. (2007) 'Institutions in international relations: understanding the effects of the GATT and the WTO on world trade', *International Organization*, 61(1), pp. 37–67. doi:10.1017/S0020818307070014. GATT/WTO increased trade.
- Ikenberry, G.J. (2018) 'The end of liberal international order?', *International Affairs*, 94(1), pp. 7–23. The order is resilient under Trump.
- Mearsheimer, J.J. (2019) 'Bound to fail: the rise and fall of the liberal international order', *International Security*, 43(4), pp. 7–50. doi:10.1162/ISEC_a_00342.
- Lake, D.A., Martin, L.L. and Risse, T. (2021) 'Challenges to the liberal order: reflections on *International Organization*', *International Organization*, 75(2), pp. 225–257. doi:10.1017/S0020818320000636.
- von Borzyskowski, I. and Vabulas, F. (2019) 'Hello, goodbye: when do states withdraw from international organizations?', *Review of International Organizations*, 14(2), pp. 335–366. doi:10.1007/s11558-019-09352-2.
- Eilstrup-Sangiovanni, M. (2020) 'Death of international organizations: the organizational ecology of intergovernmental organizations, 1815–2015', *Review of International Organizations*, 15(2), pp. 339–370. About 39% of IGOs have died.
- Mearsheimer, J.J. (2014) 'Why the Ukraine crisis is the West's fault', *Foreign Affairs*, 93(5), pp. 77–89. A contested policy essay; cite it alongside the McFaul/Sestanovich rebuttals.
- Morozov (2023) 'Russia and the liberal order: from contestation to antagonism', *International Affairs*, 99(6). doi:10.1093/ia/iiad229. Partly unverified: confirm the author's first name and the page range before citing.
