## Part A: Lecture notes

### 1. Starting points

- **There is no single feminism.** Feminist IR is a family of approaches (liberal, Marxist/socialist, postcolonial, poststructuralist, and others) that overlap and sometimes contradict each other. Write "feminist approaches", not "feminism says".
- **Shared complaint with Marxism:** the *scope* of mainstream IR is too narrow. Marxists say it ignores class and capital; feminists say it ignores gender, women, and the private sphere.
- **What unites them** is a commitment to taking **gender** seriously as a category of analysis, and a normative interest in **change** (emancipation), as opposed to explaining a supposedly timeless system.

### 2. Sex and gender

**The basic distinction**
- **Sex** = biological (e.g. pregnancy/childbearing).
- **Gender** = social (e.g. childrearing/childcare). Who looks after children is a social arrangement, not a biological fact.

**Why the distinction matters**
- Social categorisation is not automatic or neutral. It reflects the **imposition** of a particular view of the right social order.
- That order attaches **unequal values** to the two genders:
  - *Masculinity* is tied to autonomy, sovereignty, objectivity, universalism, reason and abstraction.
  - *Femininity* is defined as the **absence** of these (dependence, emotion, the particular, the body).
- Gender is therefore **relational and hierarchical**. It is a structure of power, not just a description of two kinds of people. This is what lets feminists ask how IR's own concepts are "masculine".

**The distinction is contested**
- **Judith Butler** (*Gender Trouble*, 1990): we cannot cleanly separate a natural binary *sex* from a constructed *gender*. The belief that there are two natural sexes is itself an effect of gender norms that are **performed** through repeated acts. Gender is something we do, not something we have.
- **Elizabeth Grosz and Susan Bordo** reply that Butler reduces everything to discourse and loses the **material body** (bodies that bleed, give birth, are injured, starve).
- Lecturer's steer: be aware of this debate; it is examined in more depth later in the degree.

### 3. Where are the women? The empirical critique

- Feminists argue mainstream IR **excludes** women's lives and experiences, and female scholars. The result is a partial, **"malestream"** view.
- Treating IR as the **"high politics" of war** and diplomacy:
  - hides women's role in diplomacy and international politics (often informal: wives, secretaries, activists, peace movements);
  - ignores what military action does to the **social and material survival of communities**.
- **Gender-sensitive research** sets out to change the *kind of knowledge* produced. Examples from the lecture:
  - women in **Third World development and globalisation**: women's central place in **global value chains**; women as a **flexible reserve army of labour**;
  - effects on women of **changed social policy** in industrial states (childcare, workplace insecurity);
  - **gender-specific effects of international organisations** (e.g. structural adjustment);
  - the **re-institutionalisation of rigid gender roles** in post-communist Central and Eastern Europe, and links to **new populisms**;
  - **global care chains** and the **reproductive economy** (migrant women doing care work in richer states, leaving care gaps at home);
  - **gender, militarisation and war**.
- Associated scholar to cite: **Cynthia Enloe**, *Bananas, Beaches and Bases* (1989). Asking "where are the women?" and showing that "the personal is international".

### 4. Implications: IR's key concepts are gendered

The deeper claim is not just that IR leaves women out. It is that the discipline is **built on gendered assumptions** linked to male dominance, so it *cannot see* gender.

- Core concepts (**power, sovereignty, anarchy, security**) reproduce a patriarchal **public/private divide**.
  - Male experience and knowledge sit in the **public** sphere, which counts.
  - Women's experience has been placed in the **private** sphere, which "does not count" in IR.
- **Analytical cost:** because malestream IR does not see its own gendering, it is worse at explaining **change and continuity** in world politics.
- **Rejecting the domestic/international split**, in parallel with rejecting the public/private split. The two levels are **interdependent**. Excluding the domestic (like excluding the private) is itself a gendered decision about what matters.

**Five concepts deconstructed (especially against Realism)**

| Concept | Mainstream view | Feminist critique |
|---|---|---|
| **Man** | Rational, self-maximising individual (Realism and Liberalism) | A model made *by* men, of how *some* men act, in a specific gendered context. Not universal human nature. |
| **The state** | Sovereign unit; bringer of order | The state **reflects and produces gender power**, socialising narrow gender identities into men and women. Men = public order; women = private, associated with disorder/anarchy, which is exactly what IR treats as the **source of threat**. |
| **Power** | **"Power-over"**: A gets B to do what B would not otherwise do | Rests on a masculine ideal of autonomy as being *free from* others. Feminists look at how the **"rules of the game"** are set before any power-over is exercised. Power becomes broader and more structural. |
| **Rationality** | **Instrumental**: thought and relationships as means to ends; cooperation is only ever tactical | This rationality is **disembodied and detached**, abstracting from history and from the **relationships women are made responsible for maintaining**. The real world is relational. |
| **Security** | Stability through **deterrence** under anarchy | Not adequate. Realism is itself **endangering**, because it raises a masculine concern with power-over to the supreme value of the system. |

### 5. Security

- **Shift of referent:** from the **state** to the **individual or group** (overlaps with **human security**).
  - Example: the 1950s USA was the strongest military power on earth, but it makes little sense to call a Black woman living under **Jim Crow** "secure".
- **Negative vs positive security/peace:**
  - Realists and neoliberal institutionalists: security as an **absence** of war. For **Waltz**, a nuclear balance of power that prevents great-power war counts as peace.
  - Most feminists: peace and security need **positive** content, such as international empathy, **demilitarisation**, and some level of **economic security and justice**. (Compare Galtung's "positive peace".)

### 6. Different feminist approaches

| Approach | Main focus | Strengths and problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberal feminism** | Women's roles in world politics; how women are **excluded from power**; equal rights with men | The most "common sense" version. **Problem-solving** (in Cox's sense: works within the existing order). Partly absorbed into the mainstream (e.g. World Bank on women's employment). Lecturer's critique: can slip into **gender essentialism**, e.g. "more women in banking will tame the system". |
| **Marxist feminism** | Women's oppression as a product of **capitalism** | Gender reduced to class. |
| **Socialist feminism** | **Capitalism and patriarchy** as two linked structures | Working-class women face **dual oppression**. Capitalism depends on women's **unpaid domestic labour**, which covers the cost of producing the next generation of workers (social reproduction). |
| **Postcolonial feminism** | Racial stereotypes; critique of feminism dominated by **white Western women**; stress on **plurality** | Critical of "feminism" used to **justify interventions** (e.g. Afghanistan 2001; cf. Spivak's "white men saving brown women from brown men"). Also (e.g. **Shirin Rai**) aware that **anticolonial movements** can demand silence about oppression within their own societies. A double bind. |
| **Poststructuralist feminism** | How societies construct **masculinity and femininity**, especially through **language**; how these vary across time and place | Identity as **performed**; critical of taken-for-granted "sex" (links back to Butler). |

- **Hybrids are common.** Marxist and postcolonial feminisms often overlap. The main difference is the form of domination stressed: Marxists emphasise the **material and economic**; postcolonial scholars are often as or more interested in the **psychological and cultural**.

### 7. Intersectionality

- **Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989)** coined **intersectionality**: race, class and gender inequalities **intersect** and cannot be added up separately. A Black working-class woman's experience is not "sexism + racism + classism".
- **The dilemma** for feminism (and any emancipatory theory): how to give real weight to **difference** without collapsing into **micro-level, personalised identity politics**, while still building **broad alliances** able to bring about social change.

### 8. The four take-aways (lecturer's summary)

1. **Gender is a social issue, not a biological one.**
2. **Rejection of anarchy as the structuring principle** of world politics.
3. A **broader, human-centred understanding of security**.
4. A strong interest in the **possibilities for change** in world politics.

Next week: **Social Constructivism**.

### Key terms glossary

- **Malestream**: mainstream scholarship that presents a male-centred perspective as universal.
- **Public/private divide**: the separation of politics (public, male) from the household and personal life (private, female), which feminists argue is political itself.
- **Power-over**: Dahl's definition; A's ability to make B do what B would not otherwise do.
- **Instrumental rationality**: reason as calculating means to fixed ends.
- **Gender essentialism**: the assumption that men and women have fixed natures (e.g. women are naturally peaceful).
- **Social reproduction**: the (largely unpaid, feminised) work of sustaining people and raising the next generation.
- **Global care chains**: transnational chains of paid and unpaid care work, usually women moving from poorer to richer states.
- **Performativity** (Butler): gender produced through repeated acts rather than expressing an inner essence.
- **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw): overlapping systems of disadvantage.
- **Human security**: security centred on individuals and communities rather than states.

## Part B: Seminar answers

### Overarching question: How do feminists understand power and the state?

**Short answer:** feminists treat both power and the state as *gendered*. Power is not only the visible capacity to coerce, but the structural ability to define what counts as political in the first place. The state is not a neutral sovereign container that provides security; it is an institution that produces and relies on gender hierarchy, and it can be a source of insecurity for many of the people it claims to protect.

**Power**

1. **Critique of power-over.** Mainstream IR, especially Realism, defines power as "power-over" (Dahl: A gets B to do what B would not otherwise do) and measures it in material capabilities. Feminists argue this rests on a masculine ideal of the autonomous actor whose freedom means independence *from* others. Morgenthau's "political man" is the model. **J. Ann Tickner** (1988, "Hans Morgenthau's Principles of Political Realism: A Feminist Reformulation") argues that this is a partial description of human behaviour presented as universal.

2. **Power as setting the rules of the game.** The lecture's alternative focuses on how the rules are organised *before* any act of power-over takes place. This resembles Lukes' second and third dimensions of power (agenda-setting, and shaping preferences). The public/private divide is itself an exercise of power: by placing care, the household and sexual violence in the "private" sphere, it removes them from political scrutiny. Enloe's point that the international relies on private arrangements (diplomatic wives, women around military bases, low-paid women workers in export zones) shows power running through places IR does not look.

3. **Power-to and power-with.** Some feminists draw on Hannah Arendt's idea of power as the human ability to act in concert, and on ideas of **empowerment**. Power then includes the collective capacity to cooperate, which matters because it undercuts the Realist claim that cooperation is only ever instrumental.

4. **Nuance: feminists disagree.** Liberal feminists largely accept the conventional meaning of power and ask for women's equal *access* to it (more women leaders, diplomats, soldiers). Socialist feminists locate power in the joint structures of capitalism and patriarchy (unpaid reproductive labour). Poststructuralists see power working through discourse and identity, producing "men" and "women" in the first place. Postcolonial and intersectional feminists stress that gendered power is never separate from race and class, so "women" do not all stand in the same relation to it.

**The state**

1. **The state as gendered.** Rather than starting from sovereignty, feminists see the state as **reflecting and reproducing gender power**: it socialises narrow gender identities into men and women (protector/soldier/citizen vs mother/carer/protected). Men are linked to the public realm and the creation of order; women to the private realm, which IR associates with disorder.

2. **The protection racket.** The state's claim to legitimacy rests on protecting "womenandchildren" (Enloe's phrase). Feminists argue this protective masculinity justifies militarism and makes the protected dependent, while violence against women inside the state goes unaddressed. Hence the lecture's Jim Crow example: a powerful *state* can coexist with deeply *insecure* citizens.

3. **The state's dependence on the private sphere.** States depend on women's unpaid reproductive labour (raising citizens and soldiers) and regulate it through family law, citizenship, reproductive policy and welfare. Rigid gender roles re-institutionalised in post-communist states, or championed by new populist movements, show the state actively shaping gender rather than simply reflecting it.

4. **Ambivalence about the state.** Feminists are divided on whether the state can be used for emancipation. Liberal feminists see it as a tool for rights and equality (anti-discrimination law, UNSCR 1325, feminist foreign policy such as Sweden's from 2014). Radical and poststructuralist feminists (e.g. Catharine MacKinnon's view of the state as "male", Wendy Brown's "Finding the Man in the State") are sceptical that an institution built on masculine power can deliver liberation. Postcolonial feminists are wary of states using women's rights to justify intervention abroad.

**Evaluation for a first-class answer.** The strongest version of the argument links the two: the gendered definition of power (as autonomy and coercion) and the gendered state (as sovereign protector) reinforce one another, and together they produce a narrow, state-centric, militarised concept of security. The obvious objection is that some of this is ideal-typed: plenty of mainstream scholars already use structural or relational accounts of power (e.g. Barnett and Duvall 2005, Strange's structural power), and states vary enormously in their gender regimes. A good answer would acknowledge that feminist accounts are strongest when they show *specific* mechanisms (military recruitment, care chains, citizenship law) rather than asserting that the state is male in the abstract.

### 1. What is the difference between sex and gender in feminist thinking?

**The standard distinction.** In second-wave feminism (formalised in sociology by Ann Oakley, 1972, drawing on Simone de Beauvoir's "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman", 1949), **sex** refers to biological difference (chromosomes, reproductive anatomy, the capacity to give birth) and **gender** refers to the social meanings, roles and expectations attached to being a man or woman. The lecture's example: childbearing is a matter of sex; childrearing is a matter of gender.

**Why it matters politically.** The distinction was a political weapon. If women's subordination is a matter of *gender* rather than *sex*, it is not natural or inevitable; it is a social arrangement that can change. It also exposes that gender is **hierarchical and relational**: masculinity is tied to reason, autonomy and objectivity, femininity to their absence. Once gender is understood as a set of valued and devalued qualities, it can be used to analyse things that are not about bodies at all, such as states, theories and concepts. This is how feminist IR moves from "adding women" to arguing that **IR's concepts are themselves masculine** (e.g. Carol Cohn's 1987 study of the gendered language of nuclear defence intellectuals).

**Complications.**
- **Butler (1990)** argues that sex is not a pre-social given on which gender is layered. The idea of two natural, binary sexes is itself produced by gender norms that are **performed**. On this view, "sex was always already gender". Supporting evidence includes intersex people and the history of how medicine has assigned bodies to categories (e.g. Anne Fausto-Sterling).
- **Grosz and Bordo** object that Butler loses the **materiality of the body**. Bodies have weight: pregnancy, sexual violence, illness and hunger are lived physically, and a theory that dissolves the body into discourse struggles to explain those experiences.
- **Postcolonial and intersectional critiques** add that "gender" has never been experienced the same way across race, class and culture, so the category "woman" can hide as much as it reveals (Crenshaw 1989; Chandra Mohanty, "Under Western Eyes").
- **Risk of essentialism.** Liberal feminism sometimes slides back into treating gendered traits as fixed (women as naturally more peaceful or cautious). The lecture's example, "more women in banking will tame the system", reproduces the very stereotypes the sex/gender distinction was meant to dismantle.

**Conclusion.** The sex/gender distinction is best seen as a **starting point that feminist thought has partly moved beyond**. It establishes that gender is social and changeable, which is the central claim of feminist IR (take-away 1). But most contemporary feminists treat the line between sex and gender as contested: either because sex itself is socially constructed (Butler), or because bodies still matter in ways social construction cannot capture (Grosz, Bordo). For IR, the key point is that **gender is a structure of power and meaning, not a variable describing individuals**.

### 2. Does feminism provide a distinctive approach to the study of IR?

**Argument: yes, distinctive in subject matter, ontology, epistemology and normative purpose, though it shares ground with other critical theories, and its distinctiveness varies by strand.**

**Reasons it is distinctive**

1. **Subject matter: it looks somewhere else.** Feminist IR asks "where are the women?" (Enloe) and finds world politics in places other theories ignore: the household, care work, sex work around military bases, export processing zones, global care chains, the reproductive economy. No other theory treats **gender** as its central category of analysis.

2. **Ontology: rejecting the core divides.** Feminists reject the **public/private** and **domestic/international** splits that hold up Realism and much of Liberalism. Waltz's third-image theory depends on bracketing the domestic. Feminists argue the levels are interdependent, and that excluding the private is itself a gendered choice. They also reject **anarchy** as *the* structuring principle (take-away 2), seeing hierarchies of gender, race and class as at least as fundamental.

3. **Conceptual critique: the field's tools are gendered.** Feminist IR's most original move is to show that "neutral" concepts (rational man, sovereign state, power-over, instrumental rationality, security as deterrence) encode masculine assumptions. This is more than adding a new variable; it claims mainstream theory is **partial** and so less able to explain change and continuity.

4. **Security redefined.** The referent moves from state to individual and group, and security is defined positively (economic justice, demilitarisation, empathy) rather than as the absence of war. The Jim Crow example captures why.

5. **Epistemology and method.** Many feminists use standpoint, ethnographic and narrative methods, starting from marginalised people's lives. This produced the famous **Keohane–Tickner exchange**: Keohane (1989, and again in 1998) welcomed feminist insights but asked feminists to produce testable hypotheses; Tickner's reply ("You Just Don't Understand", 1997) argued that this demand misreads feminism, which questions the positivist standards being applied to it. That mismatch is itself evidence of distinctiveness.

6. **Normative purpose.** Feminist IR is openly committed to **change and emancipation** (take-away 4), unlike the explanatory self-image of neorealism.

**Reasons to doubt its distinctiveness**

1. **Overlap with other critical theories.** The critique of state-centrism and anarchy, the focus on structure and emancipation, and the rejection of positivism are shared with **Marxism, Critical Theory and Constructivism**. The lecture itself notes the common dissatisfaction with Marxism. Socialist feminism could be read as Marxism plus patriarchy; poststructuralist feminism as poststructuralism applied to gender. Human security has non-feminist origins too (UNDP 1994).

2. **Liberal feminism is not very distinctive.** As a problem-solving theory asking for women's inclusion within existing institutions, it fits comfortably inside liberal IR and has been absorbed by bodies like the World Bank. If feminism can be co-opted this easily, its critical edge is not guaranteed.

3. **Internal plurality.** Because there is "not one feminism", it is hard to speak of *a* single feminist approach. Liberal, Marxist, postcolonial and poststructuralist feminisms disagree about ontology (material vs discursive), the state (tool vs obstacle) and even the subject ("women"). Intersectionality shows that gender is never analysed on its own, which complicates claims that gender is the master category.

4. **Explanatory reach.** Critics argue feminism is stronger at critique than at offering an alternative explanatory theory of, say, why great-power wars happen. Feminists reply that empirical work exists (e.g. Hudson et al., *Sex and World Peace*, 2012, finding that the physical security of women predicts state peacefulness), and that the demand for a "grand theory" is itself a malestream standard.

**Conclusion.** Feminism does provide a distinctive approach, but its distinctiveness lies less in any single claim than in **the lens it uses**: gender as a structure of power that runs through concepts, institutions and everyday life, connecting the personal to the international. Many of its critiques are shared with other critical theories, yet only feminism systematically asks how masculinity and femininity shape what IR studies and how. The most persuasive answer distinguishes between strands: **liberal feminism** is the least distinctive and most absorbed by the mainstream, while **socialist, postcolonial and poststructuralist feminisms** offer a more radical reworking of IR's ontology and epistemology. The challenge, as the lecture notes, is to keep the attention to difference that intersectionality demands while still building the broad alliances needed for change.
