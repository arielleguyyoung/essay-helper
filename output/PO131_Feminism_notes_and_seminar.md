## Part A: Lecture notes

### 1. Starting points

- **There is no single feminism.** There are multiple approaches to the study of gender in IR, many of which overlap or contradict each other. Write "feminist approaches", not "feminism says".
- **Shared complaint with Marxism:** like Marxists, feminist scholars are generally dissatisfied with the **scope** of IR theory.

### 2. Sex and gender

**The basic distinction**
- **Sex** = biological (e.g. pregnancy/childbearing).
- **Gender** = social, not biological (e.g. childrearing/childcare).
- The relationship between sex and gender is **contested** in the literature.

**Why the distinction matters**
- Social categorisation is not automatic. It typically reflects the **imposition** of a particular view of the right social order.
- That order attaches **different values** to the two genders:
  - *Masculinity* is associated with autonomy, sovereignty, objectivity, universalism, the capacity for reason and abstraction.
  - *Femininity* is held to be the **absence** of these qualities.

**The distinction is contested**
- **Judith Butler** contests the idea that we can draw a clear line between a natural binary sex and a socially constructed gender. For Butler, the idea of a clear binary sex distinction is itself a **product of the way gender is constructed/performed**.
- **Grosz and Bordo** argue that Butler **marginalises the body** itself, focusing only on the discursive construction of gender.
- Lecturer's steer: be aware of this debate; it is revisited in more specialised modules later in the degree.

### 3. Where are the women? The empirical critique

- Feminist IR thinkers claim **women are ignored** by mainstream IR. Women's lives and experiences are **excluded** from IR thinking and theorising, as are female scholars.
- Result: current IR research represents only a partial **"malestream"** view.
- Treating IR only as the **"high politics" of war**:
  - ignores the degree to which **diplomacy reflects the intervention of women**;
  - ignores the consequences of military action on the **social and material survival of communities**.
- Looking for women in IR leads to **gender-sensitive research** designed to change the **kind of knowledge produced** about IR. Examples:
  - the role of women in **Third World development and globalisation**: gender's key role in **global value chains**; women as a **flexible reserve army of labour**;
  - the effect on women of **changed social policies** in industrial societies (childcare, exposure to insecurity in the workplace, etc.);
  - the **gender-particular effects of the activities of international organisations**;
  - the **re-institutionalisation of rigid gender divisions** in post-communist Central and Eastern Europe (and women and the rise of **new populisms**);
  - **global care chains** and the **reproductive economy**;
  - **gender, militarisation and war**.

### 4. Implications: IR's key concepts are gendered

- Feminists argue gender relations have not been studied in IR **because the field itself is gendered**.
- The study of IR is **not neutral**. Its key concepts (**power, sovereignty, anarchy, security**) reflect a patriarchal division between **public and private**.
  - Male experiences and forms of knowledge are **inside** the public sphere.
  - Women's have historically been located in the **private** sphere, which has been thought **not to count** in IR.
- So it is not just that IR excludes women: it is **based on gender-specific assumptions linked to the dominance of men**.
- **Analytical cost:** the failure of malestream IR to see the gendering of its theory **limits its ability to explain change and continuity** in world politics.
- **Rejecting the domestic/international split**, just as the public/private split is rejected. The domestic and international are **interdependent**. Excluding the domestic, like the private, reflects gendered notions of what counts in IR.

**Five concepts deconstructed (how Realism's "non-gendered" actors reflect male assumptions)**

| Concept | Mainstream view | Feminist critique |
|---|---|---|
| **Man** | Realism and Liberalism see people as rational, self-maximising actors | Is this an accurate model of humanity, or a model made **by men** about how **some men** operate in a particular context of gender relations? |
| **The state** | A reflection of sovereignty | States are **reflective of gender power**, which acts on men and women to socialise **narrow gender identities** into them. Men = public realm, **bringers of order**; women = private thinking or **anarchy**, which in Realist and Liberal thought is the **source of threats** to the international system. |
| **Power** | **"Power-over"**: the ability of A to get B to do something B would not otherwise have done | Rests on the male-centred assumption that autonomy means being **free from** the direct influence of others. Alternative: focus on how the **"rules of the game"** are organised in advance of power-over. Power becomes a much broader and more significant thing. |
| **Rationality** | **Instrumental reasoning**: thinking as a means to an end, so relationships (and international cooperation) are always just a means to an end | This rationality is gendered: a **disembodied, detached, masculine** way of seeing that abstracts from historical context and from the **human relationships women are responsible for maintaining**. The world is not like this. |
| **Security** | Deterrence creates stability under anarchy | Rejected as an inadequate definition. Realism is **endangering**, because it elevates a male concern with power-over to the **supreme value** of the global system. |

### 5. Security

- **Shift of referent:** from the **state** to the **individual (or group)**, i.e. **human security**.
  - Example: in the 1950s the USA was by far the strongest military state in the world, but it makes little sense to describe a Black woman in the era of **Jim Crow** as secure.
- **Negative vs positive security:**
  - Realists and neoliberal institutionalists define security **negatively**, as an absence of war. For **Waltz**, a balance of power between states with thermonuclear weapons that prevented war counts as peace.
  - For most feminist scholars, security and peace need **positive** characteristics: **international empathy, demilitarisation, some level of economic security/justice**.

### 6. Different feminist approaches

| Approach | Main focus | Notes from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberal feminism** | The roles women play in world politics; how women are **excluded from power**; extending the **same rights** men enjoy to women | The most "common sense" version. More **problem-solving** than other variants. At least formally incorporated into the mainstream political agenda (e.g. World Bank and women's employment). Lecturer's critique: sometimes guilty of **gender essentialism**, e.g. "more women in banking will tame the system". |
| **Marxist feminism** | The international **capitalist** system | Sees women's oppression as a result of **capitalism**. |
| **Socialist feminism** | **Both capitalism and patriarchy** as structures to be overcome | Working-class women suffer a **dual oppression**. Capitalism can only function because the costs of producing the new generation are met through **women's unpaid labour in the home**. |
| **Postcolonial feminism** | **Racial stereotypes**; critical of feminism's domination by **white Western women**; stress on **plurality** | Critical of the use of "feminism" to **justify interventions**. At the same time, **Rai** and others are conscious of the way **anticolonial movements** can demand **silence** about real oppressions that exist. |
| **Poststructuralist feminism** | How societies shape **masculinity and femininity** (and how these differ over time and between societies), especially through **language** | How we **perform** our identities; critical of how "sex" is understood. |

- **Hybrids are common.** Marxist and postcolonial concerns often overlap. The main difference is usually the form of domination stressed: Marxism focuses on the **material/economic**; postcolonial scholarship is often as or more interested in the **psychological and cultural**.

### 7. Intersectionality

- In **1989, Kimberlé Crenshaw** coined **intersectionality** to discuss how **race, class and gender inequalities intersect**.
- **The challenge** for feminism (and any emancipatory theory): how to give proper weight to **difference** without descending into a **micro-level, highly personalised identity politics**. How to respect difference while building **broad alliances** capable of effecting social change.

### 8. The four take-aways (lecturer's summary)

1. **Gender is a social issue, not a biological one.**
2. **Rejection of anarchy as a structuring principle.**
3. A **broader, more human-centric understanding of security**.
4. A passionate interest in the **possibilities for change** in world politics.

Next week: **Social Constructivism**.

### Key terms glossary

- **Malestream**: the partial, male-centred view feminists say current IR research represents.
- **Public/private divide**: the patriarchal division that places male experience in the public sphere (which counts in IR) and women's in the private sphere (which does not).
- **Power-over**: the ability of A to get B to do something B would not otherwise have done.
- **Instrumental rationality**: thinking as a means to an end, so relationships are also means to an end.
- **Gender essentialism**: assuming fixed gendered natures, e.g. "more women in banking will tame the system".
- **Performance of gender** (Butler / poststructuralists): gender and even binary sex as products of how identities are performed.
- **Human security**: security with the individual or group, rather than the state, as its focus.
- **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw, 1989): how race, class and gender inequalities intersect.

### Overarching question: How do feminists understand power and the state?

**Short answer:** feminists see both power and the state as **gendered**. Power is not just "power-over" but the broader ability to organise the **rules of the game** in advance. The state is not simply a reflection of sovereignty but an institution **reflective of gender power**, which shapes the gender identities of the people within it.

**Power**

1. **Critique of power-over.** Most IR theory treats power as "power-over": the ability of A to get B to do something B would not otherwise have done. Feminists argue this rests on a **male-centred assumption** that autonomy means being **free from** the direct influence of others. It links to the gendered values the lecture identifies: masculinity is associated with autonomy and sovereignty, and femininity with their absence.

2. **Power as setting the rules of the game.** The feminist alternative focuses on the ways the **rules of the game are organised before** power-over is exercised. On this view, power is "a much broader and more significant thing altogether".

3. **The public/private divide as power.** IR's key concepts, power included, reflect a patriarchal division between public and private. Deciding that the private sphere "does not count" in IR is itself an exercise of this broader power. The lecture also notes that social categorisation reflects the **imposition** of a particular view of the right social order.

4. **Power, rationality and security.** Power-over is tied to instrumental rationality (relationships as means to ends) and to Realism's view of security. Feminists argue Realism is **endangering**, because it raises a male concern with power-over to the supreme value of the global system.

5. **Feminists disagree.** Drawing on the lecture's approaches:
   - **Liberal feminists** focus on how women are **excluded from power** and want the same rights extended to women. This largely accepts the existing meaning of power and asks for access to it.
   - **Marxist feminists** locate power in the **capitalist system**; **socialist feminists** in **capitalism and patriarchy** together (women's unpaid domestic labour).
   - **Poststructuralists** see power working through **language** and the **performance** of masculinity and femininity.
   - **Postcolonial feminists** and **intersectionality** (Crenshaw) stress that gendered power intersects with **race and class**.

**The state**

1. **The state as gendered.** Instead of seeing the state as a reflection of sovereignty, some gender theorists see states as **reflective of gender power**, which acts on men and women to **socialise narrow gender identities** into them.

2. **Order and anarchy.** Men are identified with the **public realm as bringers of order**. Women are identified with **private thinking or anarchy**, which in Realist and Liberal thought is the **source of threats** to the international system. Gender is therefore built into the very distinction between order and anarchy that IR rests on. This is why feminists **reject anarchy as a structuring principle**.

3. **Rejecting the domestic/international split.** Feminists reject the separation of domestic and international politics, just as they reject the public/private split. The two are **interdependent**, so what happens inside states matters to world politics.

4. **A strong state is not a secure people.** Feminists move the focus of security from the **state to the individual or group**. The 1950s USA was the world's strongest military state, yet a Black woman under **Jim Crow** could hardly be called secure. State strength and human security are not the same thing.

5. **The state and the economy.** Lecture examples show states and international organisations having **gender-particular effects**: changed social policies (childcare, workplace insecurity), and the **re-institutionalisation of rigid gender divisions** in post-communist Central and Eastern Europe and new populisms. The state actively shapes gender roles rather than standing neutral.

6. **Differences between approaches.** Liberal feminism works within existing institutions and has been partly absorbed into the mainstream agenda (e.g. the World Bank and women's employment), so it treats the state and international institutions as places to win rights. Postcolonial feminists warn about "feminism" being used to **justify interventions**.

**Evaluation.** The two ideas reinforce each other. A gendered idea of power (autonomy as freedom from others; power-over) and a gendered state (men as bringers of order, women linked to anarchy) together produce a narrow, deterrence-based idea of security. The feminist alternative is a broader view of power and a **human-centric** view of security, with positive content: **empathy, demilitarisation and economic justice**. A balanced answer should note that feminists themselves disagree about the state, from liberal feminists seeking inclusion to socialist and postcolonial feminists who see deeper structures of domination.

### 1. What is the difference between sex and gender in feminist thinking?

**The basic distinction.** At a basic level, **sex is biological** and **gender is social**. The lecture's example: **childbearing** (pregnancy) is determined by sex, but **childrearing** (childcare) is socially determined.

**Why it matters.** Social categorisation is **not automatic**. It typically reflects the **imposition of a particular view of the right social order**, and that order attaches **different values** to the two genders. Masculinity is associated with autonomy, sovereignty, objectivity, universalism, reason and abstraction; femininity is defined as the **absence** of these. So gender is not a neutral description of difference. It is a **hierarchy of values**.

This is what lets feminists apply gender to IR itself. Several of the "masculine" qualities (sovereignty, autonomy, objectivity, reason) are exactly the qualities IR assigns to states and rational actors. Feminists therefore argue IR's key concepts (power, sovereignty, anarchy, security, rationality, "man") are **gendered**, not neutral.

The distinction is also the basis of the first of the lecture's four take-aways: **gender is a social issue, not a biological one**. If gender is social, it can change, which links to feminism's interest in the **possibilities for change** in world politics.

**The distinction is contested.**
- **Butler:** we cannot clearly separate a natural binary sex from a socially constructed gender. The idea of a clear binary sex is itself a **product of how gender is constructed and performed**.
- **Grosz and Bordo:** Butler goes too far and **marginalises the body**, focusing only on the discursive construction of gender.
- **Poststructuralist feminists** follow the Butler line: they examine how masculinity and femininity are shaped (especially through language), how they vary over time and between societies, and how we **perform** identities. They are critical of how "sex" is understood.
- **Gender essentialism:** the lecture warns that liberal feminism sometimes treats gendered traits as fixed, e.g. "more women in banking will tame the system". This cuts against the point of the distinction, which is that such traits are social, not natural.
- **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw, 1989): gender inequality **intersects with race and class**, so gender cannot be analysed alone.

**Conclusion.** The sex/gender distinction is the foundation of feminist IR because it shows that gender is a **socially imposed hierarchy of values** rather than a biological fact. But the line between the two is **contested**: Butler argues sex itself is a product of gender performance, while Grosz and Bordo insist the body still matters.

### 2. Does feminism provide a distinctive approach to the study of IR?

**Argument: yes. Feminism is distinctive in what it studies, how it views IR's core concepts, and its aims. But it overlaps with other theories, and its distinctiveness varies between strands.**

**Reasons it is distinctive**

1. **It looks for women.** Feminists argue mainstream IR **ignores women**: their lives, their experiences, and female scholars. This leaves a partial "**malestream**" view. Looking for women leads to **gender-sensitive research** that changes the kind of knowledge produced. Examples include women in global value chains and as a flexible reserve army of labour, global care chains, the reproductive economy, the gendered effects of international organisations, and gender, militarisation and war.

2. **It goes beyond "high politics".** Seeing IR only as the high politics of war ignores **women's intervention in diplomacy** and the effects of military action on the **social and material survival of communities**.

3. **It shows IR's concepts are gendered.** This is the most distinctive claim. It is not just that IR leaves women out: the field is **built on gender-specific assumptions linked to the dominance of men**. "Man", the state, power, rationality and security all turn out to reflect male assumptions. Because malestream IR cannot see this, it is **less able to explain change and continuity**.

4. **It rejects key divides.** Feminists reject the **public/private** and **domestic/international** separations, treating them as interdependent. They also **reject anarchy as a structuring principle**.

5. **It redefines security.** The focus moves from the state to the **individual or group** (the Jim Crow example), and security means more than the absence of war (contrast Waltz's nuclear balance of power). It needs positive features such as **empathy, demilitarisation and economic security/justice**.

6. **It is committed to change.** Of the lecture's four take-aways, the last is a **passionate interest in the possibilities for change** in world politics.

**Reasons to doubt its distinctiveness**

1. **Overlap with Marxism.** Feminists share Marxists' dissatisfaction with the **scope** of IR theory. Marxist feminism explains women's oppression through **capitalism**, which makes it close to Marxism itself. The lecture notes extensive overlap between Marxist and postcolonial concerns too.

2. **Liberal feminism is close to the mainstream.** It is the "common sense" version: **problem-solving**, focused on extending men's rights to women, and at least formally **incorporated into the mainstream agenda** (World Bank). It can also slip into **gender essentialism**.

3. **There is no single feminism.** Feminist approaches **overlap or contradict** each other: liberal, Marxist, socialist, postcolonial, poststructuralist. Even sex/gender is contested (Butler vs Grosz and Bordo). This makes it hard to speak of one distinctive approach.

4. **The problem of difference.** Postcolonial feminists criticise feminism's domination by **white Western women** and its use to **justify interventions**. **Intersectionality** shows gender always intersects with race and class. This raises the challenge the lecture identifies: respecting difference without sliding into **highly personalised identity politics**, while still building **broad alliances** for change.

**Conclusion.** Feminism does provide a distinctive approach. Its distinctiveness comes from treating **gender as a social structure** that runs through IR's own concepts (man, state, power, rationality, security), not only from adding women to the picture. Its rejection of anarchy and of the public/private and domestic/international divides, and its human-centric view of security, set it apart from Realism and Liberalism. However, it is better described as a family of approaches than as one theory. **Liberal feminism** is the least distinctive and closest to the mainstream. **Socialist, postcolonial and poststructuralist** feminisms offer a more radical challenge, though they overlap with Marxist and other critical concerns.
