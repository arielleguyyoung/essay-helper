# Essay plan: "How convincing is neoliberal institutionalism as a theory of international politics?"

Undergraduate IR essay, about 2,000 words, Harvard referencing. Section 4 shows how to scale to 2,500 or 3,000 words. Full source details are in `output/neoliberal-institutionalism-sources.md`.

---

## 1. Decoding the question

**"How convincing"** asks for a verdict with a degree attached. "It depends" is acceptable only if you say *on what*. An essay that lists strengths, then weaknesses, and concludes that NLI "has both" usually lands in the 2:2 to low 2:1 range.

**"As a theory of international politics"** is your lever. Keohane built NLI to explain *cooperation under anarchy*, mainly in the world political economy. Judge it against its own aims (fairly strong) and against the broader claim the question puts to it (weaker). Make that distinction explicit.

**Markers expect:**
- An accurate statement of the core claims. Institutions reduce transaction costs, provide information, lengthen the shadow of the future and make commitments credible (Keohane 1984; Axelrod and Keohane 1985; Keohane and Martin 1995).
- Awareness that NLI shares neorealism's assumptions, the "neo-neo" debate (Baldwin 1993).
- The relative gains debate handled properly: Grieco's challenge, then Powell and Snidal on *when* it bites.
- Cases used as tests, not decoration.
- Engagement with recent work (2018 onwards) on the liberal order.

**Pitfalls:**
1. Describing instead of evaluating. Every body paragraph needs a sentence on what the material does to the theory's credibility.
2. Confusing NLI with neoliberal economics (Thatcher, Reagan, deregulation). Define the term in the introduction.
3. Treating NLI as liberalism in general. The democratic peace and Kant are different theories, and NLI is state-centric and rationalist.
4. Straw-manning realism. Mearsheimer says institutions have no *independent* effect, not that they never matter.
5. Making NLI unfalsifiable. State your scope conditions *before* applying them to cases.
6. Over-reading Ukraine. It is a contested case.

## 2. Thesis options

**A. Scoped verdict (recommended)**
> Neoliberal institutionalism is convincing within identifiable scope conditions: iterated, multilateral interactions among interdependent states on economic and lower-salience issues, where force is costly. It is much less convincing on high-security issues and during shifts in the distribution of power. Because it takes state interests as given, it cannot explain the social purposes that make some institutions durable and others brittle.

**B. Stronger defence:** NLI is more convincing than its realist critics allow. NATO's survival after 1991, the trade effects of GATT/WTO membership and the consolidation of Western institutions after 2022 all show institutions shaping behaviour beyond what the distribution of power predicts.

**C. Critique from outside the neo-neo debate:** NLI is a convincing theory of cooperation but an incomplete theory of international politics. It beats realism on realism's own terms but cannot account for where preferences and legitimacy come from.

**Why A:** it answers "how convincing" directly with named conditions. It also folds in C's point and uses B's evidence. B risks cherry-picking, and C risks becoming an essay about constructivism.

One-line version for the introduction: *NLI convinces as a theory of cooperation under specific conditions, not as a general theory of international politics.*

## 3. Paragraph-by-paragraph outline (2,000 words)

The essay runs from NLI's strongest ground to its weakest. Each paragraph tests one scope condition.

### Introduction (~200)
- Open with a puzzle. If anarchy makes cooperation so hard, why did NATO outlive the threat it was built to meet?
- Define NLI in two sentences (Keohane 1984; Axelrod and Keohane 1985) and separate it from neoliberal economics.
- Set criteria for "convincing": (a) does its logic survive its strongest critics; (b) does the evidence support it; (c) how much of international politics does it cover?
- Thesis A, plus a one-sentence roadmap.

### B1: Core claims as a serious answer to Waltz (~250)
- **Point:** NLI accepts anarchy and the rational state but argues the obstacle to cooperation is often uncertainty and cheating rather than clashing interests.
- **Evidence:** Axelrod and Keohane (1985) on iteration, reciprocity and the shadow of the future. Keohane (1984) on regimes being cheaper to maintain than to create. Keohane and Nye (1977/2012) on complex interdependence.
- **Sources:** Waltz 1979; Keohane 1984; Axelrod and Keohane 1985; Keohane and Nye.
- **Evaluative link:** accepting Waltz's assumptions exposes NLI to a realist reply on those terms (B3) and to a constructivist reply that rejects them (B6).
- **Watch:** keep the description tight.

### B2: Strongest ground, economic cooperation and the post-war order (~300)
- **Point:** in iterated, multilateral economic interactions, the evidence for institutional effects is strong.
- **Evidence:** Goldstein, Rivers and Tomz (2007) on GATT/WTO trade effects. Ikenberry (2001) on institutional self-binding after 1945. Ruggie (1982) as a bridge to the shared-purpose argument in B6.
- **Evaluative link:** the scope conditions are met here, so this confirms NLI within its domain but says little yet about the rest of international politics.
- **Tip:** the 2007 paper corrects Rose (2004), which found no GATT effect. Mention the dispute in one sentence.

### B3: The relative gains challenge and its limits (~300; the theoretical core)
- **Point:** Grieco's critique is the strongest realist objection, but Powell and Snidal show it holds only under specific conditions. Those conditions are your thesis's scope conditions.
- **Evidence:** Grieco (1988) on states as defensive positionalists. Powell (1991): relative gains matter when force is cheap. Snidal (1991): the concern weakens once more than two actors are involved. Baldwin (1993).
- **Evaluative link:** neither side won. The debate made cooperation conditional, which partly vindicates NLI and partly limits it.
- **Watch:** give one sentence on each finding and its condition. Do not reproduce the models.

### B4: Security institutions, with NATO as a hard test (~300)
- **Point:** realists expect institutions to matter least in security, so finding an effect there is strong support for NLI.
- **Evidence:** Mearsheimer (1994/95) argues institutions reflect power. Wallander (2000) explains NATO's survival by its adaptable assets. Keohane and Martin (1995) reply that institutions work through information and commitment, not by overriding power.
- **Evaluative link:** persistence is not the same as independent effect. NLI explains *why the form survived* better than *whether it changes outcomes when great-power interests collide*.

### B5: Power shifts and the stressed liberal order (~300; NLI's weakest ground)
- **Point:** NLI says least about what happens when power shifts and powerful states stop valuing institutions.
- **Evidence (pick two or three):**
  - Mearsheimer (2019) vs Ikenberry (2018), set directly against each other.
  - The WTO Appellate Body, paralysed since late 2019 by US blocking of appointments. The hegemon disabled its own regime.
  - von Borzyskowski and Vabulas (2019) on withdrawals. Eilstrup-Sangiovanni (2020) estimates about 39% of IGOs have died; this shows institutions are mortal, not ineffective. Check her definitions before citing.
  - Lake, Martin and Risse (2021) as a recent overview.
  - Russia–Ukraine as a contested case. Mearsheimer (2014) blames enlargement. Against that, NATO and EU cohesion since 2022 (Finland and Sweden joining NATO, sanctions) looks like resilience. Say which reading you find stronger.
- **Evaluative link:** institutions hold where members' interests stay aligned and break where a powerful member's preferences change. NLI cannot explain *why preferences change*.
- Cite Mearsheimer (2014) as a position, not as evidence. It is not peer-reviewed.

### B6: Constructivist critique, where do interests come from? (~250)
- **Point:** NLI treats interests as fixed. This is its deepest limit as a theory of international politics.
- **Evidence:** Wendt (1992) argues anarchy is what states make of it. Ruggie (1982) argues the order rests on social purpose. Barnett and Finnemore (1999) show IOs have autonomy and pathologies, so they sometimes matter *more* than NLI allows.
- **Evaluative link:** this explains the pattern in B5. Be fair: NLI is parsimonious by design, so this limits its scope rather than proving its logic wrong.

### Conclusion (~150–200)
- Answer each of the three criteria in a sentence. Logic: holds, with conditions. Evidence: strong for economics, mixed for security, weak under power shifts. Coverage: limited by fixed interests.
- Give a one-sentence verdict that matches the introduction.
- End on an implication, such as which institutions are likely to last if US commitment wavers, rather than a recap.

## 5. Counterarguments and rebuttals

| Counterargument | Who | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Institutions just reflect power | Mearsheimer 1994/95 | Concede this for high-security issues. Rebut with Wallander and with Goldstein et al. Keohane and Martin never claimed institutions override power. |
| Relative gains make cooperation fragile | Grieco 1988 | Powell and Snidal show it depends on cheap force and few actors. Turn it into your scope conditions. |
| The liberal order is collapsing, so NLI is refuted | Mearsheimer 2019 | Decline is consistent with NLI once interests change. The real gap is explaining *why* they changed. Ikenberry 2018. |
| Ukraine shows institutions cause conflict | Mearsheimer 2014 | Contested. Cohesion since 2022 points the other way. |
| Interests are socially constructed | Wendt; Ruggie | Accept this as a limit on scope. Parsimony is a deliberate choice. |
| IGOs die often | Eilstrup-Sangiovanni 2020 | Mortal does not mean ineffective, and most survive. |
| NLI is unfalsifiable | Your own critique | State the scope conditions first. The WTO Appellate Body is a case where they are met and cooperation still failed. |

## 9. Final checklist
1. The thesis gives a graded verdict in the introduction.
2. NLI is defined early and never confused with neoliberal economics.
3. Every body paragraph ends with an evaluative sentence.
4. Scope conditions are stated before cases.
5. Realists are represented at their strongest.
6. Ukraine is presented as contested, and Mearsheimer 2014 is cited as an argument.
7. In-text citations and the reference list match.
8. Page numbers are given for quotations and specific claims, and the 39% figure is checked against the original.
9. The conclusion adds a judgement rather than a recap.
10. The word count is within tolerance, and your institution's rules on AI assistance are followed.
